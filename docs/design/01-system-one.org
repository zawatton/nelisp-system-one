* Status

P0 shipped 2026-09-19: the evaluation harness, the type gate, and the stubs and
mutations that make both of them evidence rather than decoration.

P1 shipped 2026-09-19 and answers the question the project was built to ask.  A
frozen Qwen3-0.6B, pooled, with a linear head reaches *0.857* held-out against
a majority baseline of *0.500*, and ECE 0.051 falls to 0.040 after temperature
scaling.  On the compositional subset -- pairs that share their polarity words
and differ only in how those words compose -- it reaches *0.792* over 48
examples, interval [0.657, 0.883], where a bag of words scores 0.507.  So the
donor is a usable decision encoder, and what it pools is sentence meaning
rather than word identity.

Four things came out of it that this document had guessed wrong or could not
have known: mid depth beats the final layer; the learned attention pool is not
decided by these runs at all, because its result inverts when its inputs are
rescaled; the calibration step was leaking twice over in ways that produced
plausible numbers rather than errors; and the run was three times slower than
the hardware required, in two places that had nothing to do with the model.
See the P1 entry in section 6.

Numbers marked /measured/ come from this repository's own suites and can be
reproduced with =make test=.  Every other number is a vendor claim or a sibling
repository's result, and is labelled as such where it appears.

Survey date: 2026-09-19.

* 1. What this is, and what it is not

On 2026-09-15 TypeSafe AI announced =Jev=, the first model of a category they
call *System One models*: a model that takes unstructured program state as
input and returns a *typed, probabilistic decision* in a single parallel pass,
instead of emitting text one token at a time.

This document is *not* a plan to reproduce Jev.  Jev cannot be reproduced:

| artifact                         | availability                          |
|----------------------------------+---------------------------------------|
| model weights                    | closed -- hosted API, early access     |
| training method (RLCD)           | named only; no paper, no details      |
| architecture specifics           | not published                         |
| self-hosting                     | not offered, not documented           |
| public                           | API surface, Python/JS SDKs, 3 primitive types |

RLCD is expanded by the vendor as /Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated
Decisions/ and nothing further is said about it.  Any claim in this repository
to have "implemented RLCD" would be unfounded, and must not be made.

What *is* reproducible is the *category*.  The vendor's own description of a
System One model decomposes into four separable claims, and they do not share a
fate:

| # | claim                                          | reproducible here? | why                                                |
|---+------------------------------------------------+--------------------+----------------------------------------------------|
| a | output is a value of a pre-declared type       | *yes, structurally* | if the output space is an enumeration, a type error is unrepresentable, not merely rare |
| b | one parallel forward pass, no autoregression   | *yes, architecturally* | a decision head over a pooled encoder needs no decode loop |
| c | probabilities are calibrated                   | *yes in effect, no in method* | calibration is measurable and trainable by standard means; RLCD itself is unavailable |
| d | 40-200x faster, $0.042/MTok, output free       | *no*                | that is a serving-infrastructure claim about their cluster |

Claim (a) is the interesting one, because it is the one usually stated loosely.
"Cannot hallucinate" is true of a System One model in exactly one sense: it
cannot emit a token sequence that fails to parse into the declared type, because
it never emits tokens.  It can still be *wrong* -- it can pick the wrong option
with high confidence.  Calibration, claim (c), is what turns that from a defect
into a usable signal, and it is the only one of the four that requires real
research effort here.  The rest is engineering.

Claim (d) does not transfer and must never be quoted as if it did.  What can
honestly be measured in this repository is the *relative* cost of one pooled
forward pass against an autoregressive decode of the same decision on the same
substrate.  That ratio is structural -- it is the reason the vendor's number is
large -- and it survives the move to a slow interpreter, even though the
absolute latency will not be remotely competitive.

* 2. The primitives, as types

The vendor exposes three question types.  Restated as Elisp signatures:

#+begin_src elisp
;; Choice -- pick exactly one option from a declared set.
;;   Returns the chosen option, a probability for EVERY option, and a
;;   confidence.  Cardinality is capped (the vendor caps at 255).
(nso-choice STATE CRITERIA OPTIONS)
  => (:choice OPTION :probabilities ((OPTION . P) ...) :confidence C)

;; Score -- rate against ordered, described levels.
;;   The levels are ORDINAL, not merely categorical: "poor < fair < good"
;;   carries information a plain softmax over three labels discards.
(nso-score STATE CRITERIA LEGEND)
  => (:score LEVEL :probabilities ((LEVEL . P) ...) :confidence C)

;; Noul -- a yes/no question.  Returns P(yes).
(nso-noul STATE QUESTION)
  => (:p-yes P :confidence C)
#+end_src

Two observations that shape the whole design:

- *All three are the same object.*  Noul is Choice over =(yes no)= with the
  answer reported as a single probability.  Score is Choice with an ordering
  imposed on the options.  There is one head, with two specialisations, not
  three architectures.

- *Probability and confidence are different quantities and must not be
  conflated.*  The per-option probabilities are a distribution over the answer;
  confidence is a scalar about that distribution.  A uniform distribution over
  two options and a uniform distribution over two hundred both have a "most
  likely" option, but they are not equally uninformative.  Defining confidence
  as =max p= silently makes the 200-way question look catastrophically
  unconfident and the 2-way question look adequate, when the 200-way answer may
  carry far more information.  §4 fixes a definition; the point here is that it
  is a *design decision*, not a readout.

* 3. Architecture

The plan is a *frozen donor encoder plus a typed decision head*.  No
pre-training.  The sibling =nelisp-llm= repository (Doc 8, phases 0-2d SHIPPED,
phase 3 in progress) already imports Qwen3-0.6B as a 571 MiB int8 table, runs
all 28 layers on the GPU, and trains a LoRA over the frozen base.  That is the
substrate this repository consumes.

** 3.1 Why not the obvious thing

The obvious implementation is an =N=-way softmax head: a linear from the pooled
hidden state to =N= logits.  It is wrong here for a specific reason -- the
option set is *per query*, not per model.  A fixed =N=-way head would have to be
retrained for every new question, and it has no way to use the option's own text
("expired" vs "option_3").

The head therefore *scores options against the state* rather than classifying
the state into slots:

#+begin_example
  state text ──► frozen encoder ──► pool ──► s  (dim d)
  option text ─► frozen encoder ──► pool ──► o_i (dim d)   for each option

  logit_i = <W_s · s , W_o · o_i> / sqrt(d) + b(o_i)
  p       = softmax(logit_1 .. logit_N)
#+end_example

This handles a variable option set, an option set never seen in training, and
options whose text is informative -- at the cost of one encoder pass per option.
Since options are typically short and highly reusable, they cache: an option
embedding depends only on the option text, so a routing table of 20 options is
embedded once and reused for every query.  The per-query cost stays *one* state
pass, which is the property claim (b) rests on.

The 255 cardinality cap is worth adopting, not because of a kernel limit but
because it is a useful contract: it bounds the attention-free scoring loop and
makes the "embed every option" cost predictable.

** 3.2 Pooling

/Settled by P1's measurements; the original reasoning is kept because it was
wrong in an instructive way./

The first draft argued that a causal donor favours the last-token state, since
that is the position the next token is read from, and it named the learned
attention pool as the sophisticated option.  Measured, on the same task and the
same forward passes: *mean and last are close, the learned pool loses badly,
and the depth the states are taken from matters more than the pooling.*  Mid
depth (layer 14 of 28) beat the final layer for both parameter-free poolings --
0.870 against 0.761 for last, 0.848 against 0.783 for mean.  The final layer is
specialised for predicting the next token, which is not the same thing as
representing the sentence.

The attention pool, the only variant with parameters of its own, is *not
decided by P1*.  Its outcome depends on whether one fixed learning rate suits
the feature scale, and standardising its inputs swaps which depth fails --
mid 0.476 to 0.857 held-out, final 0.702 to 0.512.  Deciding it needs a
learning rate chosen per configuration, which is the hyperparameter search P1
ruled out; that belongs to P2.  When it does train it ties the best
parameter-free pooling, so it is not redundant either.  See the P1 entry in
section 6.

** 3.3 Ordinal heads for Score

Score's levels are ordered, so a plain softmax throws away the constraint that
confusing "good" with "excellent" is a smaller error than confusing it with
"poor".  The standard fix is a cumulative-link head: predict =K-1= thresholds
and read the level probabilities as differences of a monotone CDF.  Ordering is
then structural, not learned, which is the same argument as claim (a) one level
down.

P2 must show this beats the plain softmax on an ordinal task, or drop it.  A
mechanism that costs complexity and buys nothing measurable does not ship.

* 4. Calibration -- the actual work

Everything above is engineering with a known answer.  This section is not.

** 4.1 What "calibrated" has to mean

A model is calibrated if, among all the answers it gave probability 0.7, about
70% are right.  This is an empirical property of a model *and a distribution of
inputs*, which means:

- calibration is measured on *held-out* data, never on the training set;
- a model calibrated on one task's input distribution is not thereby calibrated
  on another's;
- a *claim* of calibration without a named evaluation distribution is vacuous.

** 4.2 Training and post-hoc correction

Two mechanisms, in order:

1. *Proper scoring rule as the training objective.*  Negative log-likelihood and
   Brier score are both minimised, in expectation, by reporting the true
   probability -- that is what "proper" means.  Training a decision head with
   NLL is therefore already calibration pressure; accuracy-only objectives are
   not.
2. *Post-hoc temperature scaling.*  A single scalar =T= dividing the logits,
   fitted on a held-out split.  It cannot change any answer (it is monotone, so
   argmax is invariant) and typically removes most of the miscalibration of a
   neural classifier.  Vector scaling (per-class affine) is the next step up and
   *can* change answers, so it needs its own accuracy check.

Neither is RLCD.  Both are standard, reproducible, and sufficient to support the
only claim this repository will make: /the reported probabilities are calibrated
on the stated evaluation set, to the stated ECE/.

** 4.3 The gate, and the trap in the gate

/Revised 2026-09-19 against P0's measurements.  The first draft of this section
asserted two things that turned out to be wrong in opposite directions; they are
corrected here, with the numbers that corrected them./

Expected Calibration Error is a binned statistic, so a bare ECE is not a result.
What the binning does, measured on the P0 stubs (=test/calibration-test.el=):

- *Too few samples per bin biases the estimate away from zero, not toward it.*
  Per-bin accuracy is a noisy estimate of per-bin confidence, and the summand is
  an absolute value, so the noise has nowhere to cancel.  A stub that is
  calibrated /by construction/ scores 0.0876 at =n=100= and 0.0109 at =n=4000=
  (mean over 6 seeds, 10 equal-width bins).  The earlier claim that scarce data
  biases ECE /low/ was backwards.

- *Too few bins biases it toward zero*, and that is where "a miscalibrated model
  can look calibrated" actually comes from.  =nso-stub-cancelling= is
  underconfident by 0.30 on half its inputs and overconfident by 0.30 on the
  other half; at one bin it scores *0.0005* and at ten bins *0.2945*.  Coarse
  bins let opposite errors cancel.

- *The scheme -- equal-width against equal-mass -- often does not matter at all.*
  When the gap keeps one sign across the whole confidence range, the absolute
  value comes outside the weighted sum and ECE collapses to =|mean confidence -
  mean accuracy|=, which no partition can change.  Measured on the
  4x-overconfident stub, equal-width/10, equal-mass/10, equal-mass/20 and the
  collapsed expression agree to 1e-9 (0.266760984).  Equal-width/50 comes out
  higher, at 0.269090 -- not a scheme effect but the first bullet again, one bin
  having flipped sign on noise.  So the earlier instruction to pick a scheme and
  never switch it is not wrong, but it guards the wrong parameter: *the bin count
  is the load-bearing one*, and both matter only where the sign of the gap
  changes.

- *No partition can report less than the global gap*, by the triangle
  inequality.  A bin count can therefore hide miscalibration but never invent
  it, which is the one direction in which a coarse ECE is safe to trust.

So the gate is: *ECE on a named held-out set, with the bin count, the binning
scheme and n stated beside it, plus a reliability diagram* -- and the diagram is
the part a human reads, because it is what shows a sign change that the scalar
has averaged away.

** 4.4 Negative control (mandatory)

This repository inherits a rule from its siblings: a check that has never been
seen to fail is not evidence.  Before any calibration result is believed, the
calibration gate must be run against a *deliberately miscalibrated* model -- the
simplest being the same model with its temperature set to 0.25 or 4.0 -- and it
must go red.  A calibration harness that reports "well calibrated" for both the
real model and a 4x-overconfident one is measuring nothing, and this failure is
easy to ship because the passing case looks identical either way.

The same discipline applies to the type-safety claim: the suite must contain a
head deliberately allowed to emit an out-of-set answer, and the type gate must
catch it.

* 5. What this cannot do

A System One model gives up string generation.  It cannot summarise, cannot
explain its answer, cannot write code, cannot answer an open question.  The
vendor states this plainly and it is not a limitation to be engineered around --
it is the trade that buys the single pass.

The consequence for this repository is that a System One model is a *decision
layer*, never a replacement for a language model.  Its use is the case where the
answer set is known in advance and the same question is asked many times over a
shared state.

** P1 -- Noul, end to end [SHIPPED 2026-09-19]

Frozen Qwen3-0.6B encoder + pooling + a single scalar head.  LoRA fit on a yes/no
task.  All three pooling variants measured.

Accept when: held-out accuracy beats the majority-class baseline by a stated
margin; ECE reported before and after temperature scaling; the pooling
comparison table is in this doc, loser included.

*** The answer

*A frozen 0.6B causal donor pools into something a linear head can use, and
what it carries is sentence meaning rather than word identity.*

The best configuration reaches *0.857* held-out against a majority baseline of
exactly *0.500*, interval [0.767, 0.916].  On the compositional subset -- pairs
whose two members share their polarity words and differ only in how those words
compose -- it reaches *0.792* over 48 examples, interval [0.657, 0.883], which
also excludes chance.  A bag of words scores 0.507 there.

That second number is the one P1 was extended to get.  The first run held out
ten compositional examples and could say nothing about them: the noise control
scored 0.900 on that subset with no signal in the features at all.

*** What was run

252 minimal-pair examples ("does this sentence report a successful outcome?"),
168 train / 84 held-out, *split by pair* so a sentence's near-duplicate partner
never sits on the other side.  108 of them are antonym pairs, 144
compositional.  Hidden states were kept at two depths from the same forward
pass, so both depths and all three poolings cost one encoder pass each.

No hyperparameter search: L2 = 0.05, 600 steps, lr 0.5, fixed in advance.  A
search would have needed its own held-out split to be honest, and not doing one
means no selection can have leaked.

  (0.972).  "The learned pool loses to both parameter-free poolings" was

- =n = 84= held-out.  =mid/last= at 0.857 and =mid/mean= at 0.833 are not

  between the two parameter-free poolings is not a result.
- The dataset was written for this repository rather than drawn from a corpus.
  The labels are the author's, the sentences are short and clean, and none of
  it resembles the messy program state a System One model would meet in use.
- *No LoRA.*  The phase description named one, the acceptance criterion did
  not.  A step needs forward and backward through the encoder -- the sibling
  repository measures 5.4 minutes for one at seq 1 -- so a hundred steps is a
  day of GPU against an hour for the whole frozen sweep.  Deferred explicitly.

*** Chasing the attention pool: a refuted hypothesis and the real cause

The unexplained row was =mid/attn=, which did not fit at 140 examples or at
252, on data every other configuration memorised completely.  Section 4.4 had
recorded a hypothesis -- scale: real hidden states are large, so =softmax(u . h)=
saturates on the first step and the gradient, which carries a factor
=a(1 - a)=, vanishes.  Mid-depth activations being the larger ones would then
explain why mid alone fails.

*The hypothesis was written as four predictions before anything was measured*,
with the rule that all four had to hold (=tools/attn-scale-probe.el=, which
reads the saved states and needs no GPU):

| # | prediction                                        | final  | mid    | verdict |
|---+---------------------------------------------------+--------+--------+---------|
| 1 | mid-depth states are larger                       | 2.976  | 3.725  | holds   |
| 2 | mid-depth score spread is wider                   | 0.1735 | 0.2921 | holds   |
| 3 | mid-depth softmax is nearer one-hot               | 0.1266 | 0.1350 | holds   |
| 4 | mid-depth |du| at initialisation is smaller       | 0      | 0      | *refuted* |

Three held and the hypothesis is still dead, which is the argument for writing
the predictions down first.  Prediction 1 holds at a ratio of 1.25; had that
been the only thing looked at, it would have read as confirmation.  What the
other rows say is that the mechanism is absent: a uniform softmax over these
sequences is 0.1149, and the measured peak is 0.1350, so the pool starts very
nearly flat and never saturates at either depth.  And =|du|= is *exactly zero*
at both depths at initialisation -- because the head is zero-initialised, so
=da_i = g (w . h_i) = 0= and every term of =du= vanishes.  A quantity that is
identical at the two depths cannot explain a difference between them.

That zero was the useful part.  Looking for what else differed turned up an
asymmetry that had been there since the head was written: *=attn= was the only
path in this repository fitting on raw features.*  =last= and =mean= hand
their pooled vectors to `nso-probe-fit-and-score', which standardises before
fitting; the joint model trained straight on hidden states with an RMS near 3.
Both paths used the same learning rate, 0.5.

Measured across learning rates (=tools/attn-lr-probe.el=, 200 steps):

| depth | lr    | train acc | loss   |
|-------+-------+-----------+--------|
| final | 0.5   |     0.500 | 19.28  |
| final | 0.1   |     0.500 | 18.04  |
| final | 0.02  |     0.500 | 13.66  |
| final | 0.005 |     0.500 |  4.447 |
| mid   | 0.5   |     0.500 | 14.17  |
| mid   | 0.1   |    *0.976* |  7.348 |
| mid   | 0.02  |     0.500 |  2.177 |
| mid   | 0.005 |     0.500 |  0.695 |

A cross-entropy of 19 is not a model that failed to learn, it is one that is
confidently wrong: divergence.  A loss of 0.695 is =ln 2=, a model that has
not moved.  Between them is a narrow window, and at mid depth lr 0.1 lands in
it and reaches 0.976.  So the failure was step size, not representation.

Standardising the states before the pool -- giving =attn= the conditioning the
other two always had, with statistics from the fit rows only -- confirmed it in
the sharpest available way: *it swapped which depth fails.*

| conditioning        | final/attn train | held-out | mid/attn train | held-out |
|---------------------+------------------+----------+----------------+----------|
| raw states          |            1.000 |    0.702 |          0.500 |    0.476 |
| standardised states |            0.518 |    0.512 |          1.000 |   *0.857* |

A result that inverts when the inputs are rescaled is a result about the
optimiser.  Neither row measures the pooling, so *the attention variant is
undecided* and P1's earlier claim that it loses is withdrawn.

Deciding it fairly needs a learning rate chosen per configuration on a
validation split, which is exactly the hyperparameter search P1 ruled out to
keep selection from leaking.  That is a real cost and it belongs to P2, with
the reason recorded rather than the search quietly added here.  What can be
said already: on the one setting where it trains, the learned pool is
competitive rather than redundant -- 0.857 held-out, the table's best
post-temperature ECE at 0.020, and its best antonym accuracy at 0.972.

*** What the run cost, and what it costs now

The first full encode took 39.7s per example and the probe's two attention
configurations took 54 minutes each.  Neither number was the hardware.

| change                               | before | after | measured on            |
|--------------------------------------+--------+-------+------------------------|
| batch each role's matmul over positions | 39.7s |  16.3s | one example, 28 layers |
| byte-compile this repository's lisp  | 54 min | 10 min | one attention config   |
| byte-compile the sibling lisp we run | 16.3s  | 10.0s | one example            |
| native-compile on top of that        |  7.5s  |  5.6s | 20 attention steps     |

The encode was spending its time in IPC, not arithmetic: one round trip per
(layer, role, position), three of them per matmul, 5292 per example at 7.5ms
each.  The sibling repository already had `nl-llm-wgpu-apply-seq', documented
as bit-identical to SEQ calls of the per-position version and wired only into
the backward.  =lisp/nso-encode.el= restructures the caller to use it and
=test/encode-equivalence-test.el= holds it to exact equality, not a tolerance.

The probe was spending its time interpreted.  `make compile' existed and every
run was throwing its output away.  The targets now depend on it, so there is no
window in which a stale .elc shadows an edited source.

One hypothesis was wrong and is recorded because being wrong quickly was the
useful part: the first guess at the probe's cost was allocation churn, 67,200
kilobyte vectors per training run.  Hoisting them out moved 39.6s to 40.3s.
Measuring said so immediately; reasoning would have said "fewer allocations,
therefore faster" and stopped there.

252 examples end to end: 190 minutes before, 59 after.

*** Two defects the controls caught before they reached this table

Both were found by running the probe over *synthetic noise* at full scale
(=tools/p1-fake-states.el=), where every held-out number must come out at
chance.  Neither produced an error; both produced plausible-looking numbers.

1. *The temperature was fitted on the training split's own logits.*  The head
   has already separated those, so the NLL-optimal temperature there is a
   /sharpening/ one.  On noise it returned T = 0.13 and drove held-out ECE from
   0.169 to 0.451.  The code carried a comment explaining why it was not fitted
   on held-out -- the leak was correctly avoided and a broken alternative
   chosen in its place.  Fixed by fitting on out-of-fold logits from within the
   training split.

2. *Fixing that moved the leak rather than removing it.*  The attention pool's
   direction was still trained on the whole training split, so the out-of-fold
   head scored features that had already seen the fold; =attn= alone still came
   back with T = 0.21 and ECE 0.346 while the fixed poolings reached 0.011 and
   0.073.  The featurizer is now refit per fold, pooling included.

Both are pinned by tests that fail if the old behaviour returns, and the second
test was mutation-checked against a deliberately broken implementation.

*** The control, re-run against the shipped code

The two defects above were found with earlier versions of the probe, so the
control was run again end to end after the fixes -- the same 140 rows, the same
1024 dimensions, the same two depths, with noise where the hidden states would
be.  Every held-out number must come out at chance, and every temperature must
flatten rather than sharpen.

| depth | pooling | train acc | held-out acc |        95% CI | ECE   | after T |      T |
|-------+---------+-----------+--------------+---------------+-------+---------+--------|
| final | last    |     1.000 |        0.522 | [0.381,0.659] | 0.169 |   0.011 | 20.00* |
| final | mean    |     1.000 |        0.587 | [0.443,0.717] | 0.189 |   0.073 |  15.71 |
| final | attn    |     1.000 |        0.522 | [0.381,0.659] | 0.122 |   0.014 | 20.00* |
| mid   | last    |     1.000 |        0.500 | [0.361,0.639] | 0.207 |   0.012 | 20.00* |
| mid   | mean    |     1.000 |        0.543 | [0.402,0.678] | 0.147 |   0.032 | 20.00* |
| mid   | attn    |     1.000 |        0.522 | [0.381,0.659] | 0.130 |   0.013 | 20.00* |

Four things this says:

- *No configuration finds signal in noise.*  Held-out accuracy spans 0.500 to
  0.587 and every interval contains 0.5.  The 0.870 in the results table is not
  an artefact of the pipeline.

- *The attention fix holds.*  Before it, =attn= on this same data returned
  T = 0.21 and drove ECE from 0.122 to 0.346.  It now returns a flattening
  temperature and 0.122 -> 0.014, like every other row.

- *Every row memorises the training split completely.*  Train accuracy 1.000 on
  pure noise is the precondition for the calibration trap: the probe has ample
  capacity, the logits come out confident, and only the held-out column knows
  otherwise.

- *A temperature at the bound is the correct answer here.*  With no signal the
  honest distribution is flat, so the optimum is past 20 and the search returns
  its ceiling.  Five rows do.  This is why the figure is marked rather than
  printed as a fit.

One row was worth flagging at the time.  On noise, =mid/attn= trains to
1.000; on the real mid-depth features it reached 0.532 at 140 examples and
0.500 at 252, never fitting at either size.  /Resolved: the cause was the
learning rate meeting unstandardised features, not the features themselves --
see "Chasing the attention pool" above.  The scale hypothesis sketched in the
rest of this paragraph was measured and refuted./
So the failure there is not a capacity limit of the model class -- random
features of the same shape fit perfectly.  The plausible cause is scale: real
hidden states carry magnitudes in the hundreds, so =softmax(u . h)= saturates
to a near one-hot pool on the first step and the gradient through it vanishes,
where noise features in [-0.5, 0.5] never saturate.  That is a hypothesis, not
a measurement, and normalising the pool scores before the softmax would test
it.  P2 should either do that or drop the learned pool.

And one caution the control illustrates better than the results table does:
on *pure noise*, =final/last= scores *0.900* on the compositional subset.  With
n = 10 a single example is ten points, and a striking number there means
nothing at all.


** P2 -- Choice and Score

Option-scoring head with a variable option set (=N= up to 255); ordinal
cumulative-link head for Score.

Accept when: Choice works on an option set held out from training (this is the
whole point of scoring options rather than classifying into slots); the ordinal
head beats a plain softmax on an ordinal task, /or/ is dropped and this doc says
so.
*** Choice: NOT ACCEPTED, and the reason is not the one the numbers first said

The head is built (=lisp/nso-choice.el=), the dataset is built
(=data/choice-intent.eld=: 12 topics, 144 requests, 4 topics held out entirely
-- sentences and option text alike, with no lexical overlap between a sentence
and its own topic name), and the measurement was run.  Choice does *not* clear
the acceptance criterion.

| evaluation             |  n | options | accuracy |        95% CI | chance |
|------------------------+----+---------+----------+---------------+--------|
| seen topics, training  | 96 |       8 |    0.385 |            -- |  0.125 |
| held-out topics        | 48 |       4 |    0.104 | [0.045,0.222] |  0.250 |
| held-out vs all topics | 48 |      12 |    0.042 | [0.012,0.140] |  0.083 |

*** What the first numbers were, and why they were not an answer

The first run reported 0.250 held out on four options -- exactly chance -- and
0.000 against twelve.  It would have been easy to write that up as section
3.1's architecture failing.  One number forbade it: *training* accuracy was
0.250 on an eight-way task where chance is 0.125.  The head had not fitted the
data it was fitted on, and a model that did not train says nothing about
whether its design generalises.  That is the same trap P1 fell into over the
attention pool, one phase earlier.

Diagnosed (=tools/p2-fit-probe.el=), the shipped setting had diverged:

| states       | lr    | train acc | loss  |
|--------------+-------+-----------+-------|
| raw          | 0.5   |     0.219 | 27.8  |
| raw          | 0.02  |     0.521 | 2.525 |
| standardised | 0.5   |     0.542 | 1.844 |
| standardised | 0.001 |     0.188 | 2.694 |

A cross-entropy of 27.8 is divergence.  So the first P2 table measured an
optimiser, not an architecture, and it is not reported above.

*** Why the head still does not fit after that is fixed

Two geometries, measured on the encoded states:

| embeddings | mean pairwise cosine | min   | max   |
|------------+----------------------+-------+-------|
| options    |                0.683 | 0.572 | 0.848 |
| states     |                0.923 | 0.859 | 0.966 |

The states sit in a narrow cone.  The Noul head of P1 survived that because a
binary decision needs one direction; separating eight does not.  Standardising
centres the cone away and is now what the probe does, which lifts training
accuracy from 0.250 to 0.385 -- real signal at three times chance, and still
not a fit.

The synthetic suite reproduces the geometry exactly (mean cosine 0.923, RMS 4)
and *fits it completely*: standardised, the head reaches 1.000 there against
0.487 raw.  So the remaining gap is not scale and not conditioning.  It is
that the donor's pooled request states and its pooled topic-label embeddings
do not stand in the relation this head assumes -- the state does not point at
its own option's direction the way a synthetic state does.

*** One hypothesis tested and refuted

The below-chance held-out accuracy invited an obvious explanation: the
option-only term =c . o_i= is a score for an option with no reference to the
state, a learned prior expressed through embeddings rather than indices, and
such a prior is arbitrary on options it has never seen -- arbitrary in a fixed
direction, which is how a head lands below chance rather than at it.

Freezing =c= at zero (=NSO_P2_FREEZE_C=, an ablation now in
`nso-choice-train') changes nothing: held-out stays at 0.104 either way.  The
hypothesis is refuted and the below-chance behaviour comes from the gate
itself.

*** What this does and does not establish

- It does *not* establish that option-scoring fails as an architecture.  The
  synthetic suite still shows the property that motivated section 3.1: a head
  fitted on five option directions reaches 1.000 on five it has never seen,
  and 0.942 on twelve.  The principle works where the state genuinely points
  at its option.
- It *does* establish that a frozen Qwen3-0.6B, pooled by the P1 recipe, does
  not produce request states and topic-label embeddings that live in such a
  relation.  Pooling a two-word label and a ten-word request through the same
  causal encoder and expecting a diagonal bilinear form to align them was an
  assumption, and it is the assumption that failed.

What would test it properly: pooled option embeddings built from descriptions
rather than two-word labels; a shared projection with more than diagonal
capacity, which needs more than 144 examples to fit; whitening rather than
per-dimension standardisation, since the cone is a covariance structure and
not a per-axis one.  Each is a phase of its own.

*** The second time a synthetic suite hid a real-scale failure

=test/choice-test.el= passed 9 of 9 -- including unseen options at 1.000 --
while the shipped setting diverged on real features.  It used unit-norm
directions; the real states have RMS 4 and sit in a cone.  P1's attention pool
was the first instance of exactly this.  Twice is a pattern, not luck, so the
suite now carries a realistic-scale case that reproduces the geometry and
pins what actually happens there.

One assertion in that new case was written before measuring and was wrong: it
demanded that raw features blow the loss up threefold, and the synthetic only
loses half the fit rather than diverging outright.  It now asserts the effect
that reproduces.

*** Score

Not started.  Choice is the acceptance criterion's first clause and it is not
met; taking the ordinal head on before the state/option geometry is understood
would be building on a foundation this phase has just found unsound.

** P4 -- integration with anvil (OUT OF SCOPE)

Route =anvil-orchestrator= model selection through a Choice, gate on confidence,
fall back to the existing path below threshold.

*Out of scope.*  Section 8 settles the definition of done as (A), the spike,
which ends at P3.  P4 is recorded here so that the earlier phases are shaped to
leave it reachable -- not so that it can be started.  Reaching it requires a new
decision and a revision of this document.

** Cross-cutting: NeLisp portability

The CPU path should run under the NeLisp standalone reader, as =nelisp-llm='s
does.  The known gaps and their absorbed workarounds are already catalogued
(=nelisp-llm/lisp/nl-llm-compat.el=); the one that matters most here is that
NeLisp's =exp= returns NaN for very negative arguments, which silently turns a
masked softmax into NaN while max-accumulating test helpers report
=PASS maxdiff=0.00e+00=.  Any test helper written in this repository counts NaN
as a failure from its first line.

* 8. Definition of done -- settled: (A) spike

Settled 2026-09-19.  The two readings that were open:

- *(A) Spike.*  Done = the method demonstrably works at toy scale, with honest
  numbers including the negative ones.  P0-P3.  The deliverable is knowledge.
- *(B) Production.*  Done = anvil's routing actually runs through this, with a
  fallback path and an operational confidence threshold.  P0-P4, and P4 carries
  reliability work not costed above.

*(A) is the scope of this project.*  It is complete when P3's acceptance
criterion is met, and it is complete whether the answer is encouraging or not: a
spike that establishes "a 0.6B causal donor does not pool into a decision
encoder good enough to be worth calibrating" has met its definition of done, and
that result gets written up here rather than worked around.

Two consequences that bind the work:

- *No production obligations are assumed.*  Latency budgets, failure modes,
  rollback paths and operational thresholds belong to (B).  Building them anyway
  is scope creep and costs the spike the speed that is its only advantage.
- *No production claims may be made.*  Nothing here may be described as ready to
  route anvil traffic on the strength of P0-P3.

Reopening the question is allowed and is a new decision, recorded by revising
this section -- not something an encouraging P3 result does on its own.
